**Numberia: Storywild — reading adventure proposal**

Prepared September 13, 2026. This is a product and implementation plan, not a shipped feature. The chosen direction prioritizes a fun, interactive reading experience; model choices serve that experience. Plan for English first, K–5 interest levels, narrator-following highlighting, and optional child-following support. Existing math adventures, progress, and earned items remain intact.

Storywild is a living storybook reached through a new door in Numberia’s clubhouse. A mischievous gust has scattered the stories: stage actors have forgotten their lines, letters have lost their destinations, and creatures need help finishing their adventures. Children become Storykeepers. Reading gives them the information and abilities to change the world.

Milo explores with the reader, Pip builds the things they describe, and Lumi becomes a gentle reading light beneath the current word. A new ink dragon, Dot, turns completed stories into miniature scenes for the child’s bookshelf. Characters celebrate specific accomplishments: “You worked out that word!” or “You found the clue in the story!”

The central promise is **read something, understand it, make something happen**. Reading a sentence directs an actor. Understanding a clue opens a route. Learning a word lets a child use it in a new story. Rewards belong to the story’s consequences.

The child is the story’s director. After reading a line, they can see it performed, replay it in a character voice, or change an unlocked word and discover how its meaning changes the scene. A taught change from “Pip hops” to “Pip skips” produces a different performance. The scene acts only after the phrase or a deliberate Continue action, keeping the words steady while the child reads. Microphone success is never the sole way to make the story respond. The first release should prove this complete reading-to-action loop in one delightful episode before adding a large map of activities.

The experience should invite children back through curiosity, competence, familiar characters, and creative ownership. Completed episodes have satisfying stopping points. There is no lost streak, unhappy pet, speed leaderboard, or expiring reward for missing a day.

An episode starts with a short character problem, introduces or revisits a useful skill, includes a connected passage, asks the child to use its meaning, and ends with a small creation or discovery. Initial pacing targets are roughly 6–10 minutes, adjustable to the child; these are product hypotheses to test. Connected reading should occupy the largest part of the episode, rather than being crowded out by reward animations.

| Story space        | What the child actually does                                                                                                                                    | Reading purpose                                                   |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |

These spaces have different actions, staging, and camera language. Sound Lagoon uses tactile shells; Puppet Parade is a theatre; Lantern Detectives is an explorable diorama; Sky Post is a delivery desk and sky route. They should not repeat the four-answer meadow with new textures. Younger readers receive fewer controls and shorter passages; older readers receive richer plots and age-respectful art, even when practicing an earlier decoding skill.

For example, in **Pip’s runaway hat**, a beginning reader works on a short-vowel pattern and already-taught consonants. Milo models a sound blend, the child builds a word, and then reads a teacher-reviewed short passage using those patterns. A line such as “Pip can hop.” makes Pip hop when the child finishes or selects Continue. A story clue explains where the hat went; the child uses that clue to help Pip. Finally, the child rereads a favorite line for the puppet performance and receives the hat as a clubhouse keepsake. Every actual sentence, character name, and irregular word must pass the episode’s decodability review; illustrative copy is not automatically suitable for every beginner.

The older-reader version can become a wind mystery with a short weather text, useful word parts, competing explanations, and a choice supported by evidence. Difficulty changes through authored content and support, while the child keeps an interesting story.

The instructional foundation comes from IES/What Works Clearinghouse practice guides. They offer recommendations with differing evidence ratings, rather than one prescribed app sequence. The game design and proposed mastery thresholds are our interpretation and require evaluation; the app should not describe itself as IES-approved or scientifically proven.

The curriculum needs an explicit, reviewed scope and sequence. Begin with phoneme blending and segmenting linked to a small set of graphemes; progress through simple words, digraphs, vowel patterns, endings, syllables, and meaningful word parts. Teach irregular portions of frequent words explicitly. Record prerequisite patterns and any pre-taught exceptions for each passage. Illustrations explain meaning after decoding; they should not teach children to guess a printed word from its first letter or picture.

Maintain two complementary content tracks: passages children can decode using taught patterns, and richer stories/information read with an adult or narrator to develop language and knowledge. Listening comprehension does not count as independent decoding. K–1 emphasizes foundations with oral comprehension, grades 2–3 expand fluent reading and meaning, and grades 4–5 extend vocabulary, morphology, inference, and informational reading. Actual practice follows demonstrated needs, not a grade label alone.

A short, friendly starting activity samples letter–sound knowledge, word reading, connected reading, and meaning at appropriate levels. A grown-up can choose or adjust the starting point. Track separate learning strands rather than calculating a reading age from one microphone attempt. Each skill stores introduced, practiced-with-help, independent-use, and later-review observations. Revisit a pattern in unfamiliar words and a new passage before treating it as established. Promotion rules should be configurable and reviewed with a literacy educator.

Help follows a consistent routine: allow thinking time; offer “Let’s work it out”; reveal the relevant graphemes or word parts; play a reviewed sound model; blend together; reread the whole word and sentence. Never replace instruction with a buzzer. Helpful retries earn the same episode reward, while the learning record distinguishes supported practice from independent evidence. Uncertain microphone recognition records no negative evidence.

Phonics sounds need their own reviewed recordings. Generic TTS can pronounce a letter name or add an unwanted vowel when asked for an isolated consonant. Use educator-checked sound clips and blending demonstrations rather than asking a general voice model to improvise phoneme instruction. Later phoneme-level scoring would need a separately validated model; word ASR alone cannot provide it.

Return visits offer an unresolved story, a favorite character’s new letter, a newly usable word, a rereading performance, or another object for the child’s story shelf. Reviewed story branches and alternate perspectives give familiar passages a reason to be read again. Replays stay available after rewards have been claimed. Shared gems can buy reading-themed decorations, while achievements name the skill or story accomplishment rather than the number of taps.

The reader should feel like a playable picture book. Use vivid coral, sky blue, golden yellow, and plum in the scene, with a stable warm reading surface and dark text. Keep Numberia’s playful display font for titles and controls; choose clear, tested letterforms for passage text. Begin testing passage sizes around 24–32 px for early readers and 20–26 px for older readers, with adjustable size and spacing. These are design starting points, not scientific prescriptions.

On wide screens, place the stable text beside the animated stage; on phones, use a small stage above one paginated passage segment and reachable controls. Keep illustrations and controls visible without shrinking text to fit. Ordinary play should avoid nested scrolling, while browser zoom and accessibility reflow remain usable. Words must be real selectable DOM text, not drawn inside WebGL. Quiet the background during active reading. A soft underline and Lumi’s position identify the current word without repeatedly resizing or bouncing it; color is not the only cue. Scene action and celebrations occur at meaningful boundaries. Reduced motion and a lightweight illustrated fallback preserve every task.

Children have three clear ways to read: **Hear the story**, **Read together** (model, then echo), and **My turn**. They can also read silently and tap Continue. Microphone access is optional. A child can tap any word for help, replay a phrase, return to the previous page, and stop listening. Automatic page turns must never jump ahead on a partial transcript.

Word highlighting has two separate implementations:

| Mode                | Recommended approach                                                                                                                                                           | Accuracy boundary                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Follow the narrator | Prepare approved narration in advance, align its actual audio to the exact passage, and ship the audio with word timing metadata. Drive the underline from the playback clock. | Timings are generated and reviewed, not estimated by dividing sentence duration. Audio and timing files must remain version-matched. |
| Follow the child    | Recognize microphone audio, then match the evolving transcript to nearby words in the current passage.                                                                         | Recognition can revise itself or miss child speech. A moving cursor is assistance, not proof of correct pronunciation or mastery.    |

Nari’s documented TTS response is PCM or WAV audio; it does not document word timing output. Keep the existing private gateway for character guidance and use Nari, or a recorded voice, to prepare story narration during content production. Run a forced aligner such as WhisperX’s alignment component against the known, verified transcript, review the output, and publish an audio file plus `{wordId, start, end}` metadata. Generate a sentence or page as a coherent performance rather than one API request per word. The child downloads finished assets; no alignment model runs before a story can start. [Nari speech format](https://docs.narilabs.com/generate-speech), [WhisperX alignment](https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX).

Alignment cannot prove a generated voice said the right words. Listen to each published clip and correct omitted, added, or mispronounced words before alignment approval. Store display text, spoken text, token IDs, text hash, voice revision, and audio hash together. Contractions, punctuation, abbreviations, and written numbers need explicit token mappings. The existing math narration normalizer is not automatically appropriate for reading passages. Seeking to a word, slowing playback, pausing, and resuming all use the same media clock. An unavailable timed asset falls back to sentence-level guidance rather than invented word accuracy.

For local child tracking, **Sherpa-ONNX is a credible candidate**. It has browser WebAssembly examples, including streaming English Zipformer and an official build workflow using an int8 encoder. Some recognizers expose token timestamps, which are not automatically complete word start/end intervals. The runtime, chosen model, tokenizer, and browser build must be tested together. [Official browser demos](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/wasm/hf-spaces.html), [English quantized build](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/blob/master/.github/workflows/wasm-simd-hf-space-en-asr-zipformer.yaml), [recognition result fields](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/blob/master/sherpa-onnx/csrc/online-recognizer.h).

Load local recognition only when Read together or My turn requests listening. Fetch pinned model assets with real byte progress, cache them for later visits, and run inference in a Web Worker. Capture and resample microphone audio outside React’s render work, with an AudioWorklet feeding bounded buffers. Check browser support and storage availability. A small “Lumi is getting ready to listen” panel can accompany setup, but the story remains usable immediately through listening and tapping. Do not promise a model download size, first-load time, or word latency before benchmarking the chosen build on target devices.

Nari also documents **`qwen3-asr-fast:free` streaming recognition** over WebSocket. It accepts 16 kHz mono PCM16; our TTS output is 24 kHz, so the audio paths must have separate format handling. Partial transcripts replace earlier hypotheses, and completed transcripts finalize an utterance. Its current schema provides utterance/VAD timing, not word timestamps. It can still feed the passage matcher for approximate live word progress. This would require a private backend WebSocket proxy; the existing HTTP TTS route is not a recognition endpoint. [Nari realtime transcription](https://docs.narilabs.com/transcribe-audio), [partial revision behavior](https://docs.narilabs.com/transcripts-and-turn-detection).

My proposed architecture is **prepared, aligned narration as the dependable core; local Sherpa recognition as an optional reading companion; a Nari recognition adapter available for comparison or explicitly enabled cloud use**. The local model must earn its place through child-speech and device testing. If it fails that test, ship excellent narrated and tap-assisted reading while deciding on the recognition provider. Do not silently upload a child’s voice when a local model fails. Ask for microphone access only on the listening action, clearly state whether audio leaves the device, show a listening indicator, and stop tracks when the activity closes. Retain derived learning observations rather than recordings by default.

As documented on the research date, Nari’s free tier has 100 accepted TTS requests per day for the fast model and 100 accepted STT connections per day for the fast recognition model, with a default shared concurrency of two. One STT connection can contain several utterances; reconnecting for each word would waste the allowance. The free tier has no latency guarantee and cannot be the assumed capacity for a classroom release. Prepared story audio avoids per-reading TTS usage. [Nari limits](https://docs.narilabs.com/rate-limits).

The child-following matcher needs explicit behavior for pauses, repetition, skipped words, restarts, and self-correction. Normalize case and punctuation while preserving meaningful word endings; do not turn “cats” into “cat.” Match in a bounded window around the current position, hold unstable hypotheses softly, and commit progress only when evidence stabilizes. A repeat must not move the reader to the next identical word elsewhere on the page. Offer a manual “start here” action when the child rereads an earlier line. Keep skipped or uncertain words distinct from confirmed matches.

Do not force-align the expected passage to a child’s recording and treat every aligned word as correctly read. Avoid passing the entire expected passage as a recognition prompt, which can encourage a model to supply words the child did not say. Keep the narrator and microphone mutually exclusive so the app cannot recognize its own voice as the child. ASR can erase or normalize reading miscues; research specifically examines this problem. Therefore an uncertain result should say “I didn’t catch that—let’s try together,” and never remove rewards or assign a pronunciation score. [Research on verbatim reading transcription and miscues](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/smith25_interspeech.html).

Integration follows the existing React ownership patterns. Add a **Reading adventures** navigation entry and lazy-loaded feature. Reuse the profile, characters, sound preference, rewards, clubhouse, and shared visual components. Reading owns its episode flow and learning state instead of extending the math `Skill` union or making `Quest.tsx` handle every reading activity.

Each episode manifest identifies its content revision, interest band, prerequisites, taught patterns, vocabulary, passage tokens, supported reading modes, narration assets, comprehension prompts, scene actions, and reward. Authored scene actions trigger from semantic events such as phrase completion or a supported comprehension choice; they do not scrape DOM text. A pure state machine separates model preparation, instruction, listening, child reading, help, meaning, and celebration. Session IDs discard stale recognition events. Leaving, changing pages, muting narration, or switching to help cancels the appropriate audio work. A scene never awards XP directly.

Reading progress lives alongside math progress, with explicit migration defaults, reset/export behavior, and bounded observations. Interest grade is separate from skill readiness. Existing math chapter unlocks keep their current meaning. Both subjects can contribute to the clubhouse through shared reward actions without duplicating a claimed episode reward.

Implementation should proceed in this order:

1. **Prove the reader and voice behavior.** Use one reviewed passage in each of three bands: K–1, 2–3, and 4–5. Prepare and inspect word-aligned narration. Compare a pinned quantized Sherpa build with Nari recognition on permitted child-speech examples and realistic pauses, noise, repeated words, and miscues. Record actual download size, warm/cold startup, highlight delay, and incorrect word advances. Adult or synthetic audio can test plumbing but cannot establish child-reading reliability.
2. **Build one complete Storywild episode.** Deliver the clubhouse entrance, reading hub, Pip’s hat story, explicit word instruction, connected passage, meaning activity, aligned highlighting, word help, a saved keepsake, and a replay. Provide authored versions for the three bands. Prove the full experience on touch and keyboard before building additional environments.
3. **Integrate persistence and supported microphone mode.** Add migration and reward tests, microphone controls, the provider adapter selected by the benchmark, uncertainty handling, and personal review recommendations. Keep no-microphone play complete. Reading help must work during a model download or service failure.
4. **Expand the reviewed curriculum and distinct activities.** Build the other story spaces with enough texts for practice and transfer. Add recurring knowledge topics, performance rereading, and creator activities. A literacy educator reviews teaching sequences, passage decodability, phonics recordings, prompts, and support before content is published.
5. **Pilot and tune for learning and enjoyment.** Observe children using it, improve confusing actions, and review whether learned patterns transfer to unfamiliar words and passages. Measure voluntary rereading, meaningful reading time, comprehension, help usefulness, and incorrect ASR interventions. Session length and return visits alone do not demonstrate reading improvement.

Acceptance checks include narrator timing against manually inspected word boundaries, pause/seek/speed synchronization, no stale highlights after a page switch, correct handling of transcript revisions and repeated words, and no progress awarded from the app’s own audio. Set a prototype narrator timing target around 150 ms at inspected word starts, then measure it; this is a design target, not a model guarantee. Child tracking must report the rate of false advances and false correction prompts, not just overall transcription accuracy.

Also verify a complete episode with the microphone denied, speech unavailable, reduced motion, keyboard only, and the smallest supported viewport. Controls and text must remain readable during celebrations and help. Old math saves must load unchanged, repeated completion must not duplicate rewards, and cached story assets must work without an inference request. Use Oxfmt, Oxlint, the existing TypeScript/tests/build checks, and focused browser checks for the implementation.

The plan is ready to become a first complete episode. The key unresolved engineering choice is which recognition model meets child-speech and device requirements; it should not delay the prepared, word-highlighted story reader that makes the experience usable from its first release.
